# Atlas Pitch — Narrative Notes

> Source: Wolfgang's notes from the July 2–3 product narrative sessions. Working material for the YC pitch and demo storytelling. Not a final script — a reference of key framings to draw from.

---

## The Why-Now (the problem statement)

Students already do their coursework with AI — **88% of them** — and almost all of it happens where teachers can't see it. The submitted artifact no longer proves learning, and **teachers have become detectives instead of educators**. The LMS, the system supposedly holding the learning, records none of this.

**Atlas is an LMS rebuilt around that reality.**

## The One-Liner

> Professors program the AI their students learn with — and see the learning, not just the submission.

## Assignment Stances: AI-Expected vs AI-Banned

Every assignment declares its stance — **AI-expected** or **AI-banned** — and for AI-expected assignments, the teacher defines the contract:

- **Hint depth** — how far the AI can go
- **Boundaries** — what it can and can't do
- **Journey rubric** — what the reasoning process, not just the final answer, is graded against

This is the control surface. The teacher isn't banning AI or surrendering to it — they're **programming the terms of engagement.**

## Integrity Without Policing (the structural argument)

When the **reasoning journey is the graded deliverable**, copying a final answer earns nothing. You solve the cheating crisis **without detection, without policing** — the assignment format itself makes cheating structurally pointless in the open lane.

This is the bridge between:
- **Positive framing:** "AI-enabled learning" — the optimistic vision
- **Integrity panic:** the why-now urgency that makes this company necessary

The integrity panic isn't a separate problem you also solve — it's the **same architecture** viewed from the integrity angle.

## Reframe: The Course Record Is the Substrate

**Don't say:** "Atlas can still include the standard LMS features." That reads defensive and invites *"so you're rebuilding all of Canvas?"*

**Do say:** The course record isn't a legacy feature set you also happen to have — it's the **substrate the agent requires**. The agent is only trustworthy because it operates on the actual record of the course. Without the LMS bones, there's no grounded context for the agent to work within.

The LMS features aren't a concession to the past — they're the **epistemological foundation** that makes the agent legitimate.

## Two Visibility Modes (critical distinction)

Without this line, "teachers see everything" reads as surveillance — the thing students (rightly) revolt against.

1. **AI-native assignment:** The work session **is** the submission — full teacher visibility, same as reading an essay draft. It's like submitting your ChatGPT chat transcripts — but structured, rubric-graded, and integrated into the course record.

2. **Open studying:** The teacher sees **aggregated signals**, not private transcripts. Students keep their exploration space; teachers see patterns (struggle areas, engagement, depth) without reading every message.

> The analogy: *"It's like submitting your ChatGPT chat transcripts"* — but make it clear this is for the AI-native assignment context, not all student-AI interaction.

## Demo Design Constraints

> ⚠️ **DO NOT create AI-generated content in the demo.** Creation-by-AI is the single most commoditized image in edtech. Showing "look, the AI wrote an essay / made a quiz / generated a lesson plan" is exactly what every edtech startup does. Atlas's story is about **programming the AI and seeing the learning** — the demo must show the *teacher's control surface* and the *student's journey visibility*, not AI content generation.

## Credibility Footnote (for the written application — not the video)

Professors are already doing primitive versions of this **by hand**:

- **Mike Kentz — "Grade the chats":** Educators are literally grading ChatGPT transcript screenshots from students. It's manual, painful, and disconnected from any system — but teachers find it more honest than grading a polished artifact they can't trust. This is the duct-tape version of journey rubrics.
- **NYU Stern — AI-conducted oral exam:** Ran an AI-administered oral exam at **$0.42/student** with grade reliability of **α = 0.86** (Cronbach's alpha). This proves the mechanism works at scale, at negligible cost, with statistical rigor — without a human in the loop for grading.

**The point:** You're not inventing a weird behavior. You're making an existing desperate workaround **native**. That's the best kind of product claim — the market has already validated the need through improvised, last-resort practices. Atlas is what those practices become when the LMS stops pretending AI isn't happening.

This is written-application material, not video material — it grounds the claim with evidence for reviewers who read carefully.

## Narrative Arc (for pitching)

1. **Problem:** Students use AI everywhere, teachers can't see it, the artifact doesn't prove learning. Teachers are detectives, not educators.
2. **Insight:** The LMS should be rebuilt around this reality — the AI is the interface, the course record is the substrate.
3. **Mechanism:** Teachers program the AI (stance + contract + journey rubric). The agent works with students under those terms.
4. **Integrity:** Journey-graded assignments make cheating structurally pointless — no policing needed.
5. **Visibility:** Teachers see the learning (session-as-submission for assignments; aggregated signals for open study).
